\begin{abstract}
An entity extractor that could point to its own likely errors would let a human review only those predictions. We
asked whether the attention a predicted entity receives can play that role, in three families of extractors: a span
scorer (\gliner{}), a token classifier (BERT) and generative decoders (Qwen2.5). On biomedical text, ranking
predictions by attention first removed about \rrGeniaMassBase\% of the risk of abstaining at random. But attention
rows sum to one, so the share a span receives depends on its size before anything the model computes. We derived the
attention an indifferent model would give a span. It is exact and parameter-free: the fraction of the visible tokens
the span occupies. Against this reference, attention is mostly geometry. Wherever attention ranked errors better than
chance, the attention-free fraction did so by about as much or more, and attention's apparent value, sign included, is
inherited from how span and sentence length relate to correctness in each corpus. Beyond geometry, attention adds
almost nothing to the model's own confidence, in every reading, extractor, architecture and error type we examined. We
then tested the mechanism on an unexamined corpus, BC5CDR, with a prediction declared beforehand. The sign was
predicted for \predPoneOk{} of \predN{} extractors, but the full prediction held for only \predConfirmed{}, so the
mechanism as stated does not transfer unchanged; there, too, attention never beat the fraction or improved confidence.
Comparing with the fraction needs only two lengths and should precede any use of attention as evidence about errors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Entity extractors are rarely trusted on their own. In practice their output passes through a human
who corrects it, and that review is what makes such systems expensive. The cost would fall if the
extractor could say which of its predictions deserve a second look. Selective prediction gives this
idea a precise form~\citep{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective}: rank the predictions by how
likely each is to be correct, deliver the top of the ranking, and send the rest to a reviewer. The
quality of the whole arrangement then rests on a single choice, the score used to rank.

The default score is the model's own confidence. We set out to test a less obvious candidate:
attention. In a transformer, each token weighs every token it can see, and the weights decide how much of each
token's representation it takes in; the weights of one token are positive and sum to one~\citep{Vaswani2017}.
The motivation is intuitive. If a model processes an entity differently when it gets it
wrong, the attention the entity receives might reveal it. Attention is also cheap to read and is
defined for every token, and attention-derived quantities have been reported to anticipate errors of
language models~\citep{yuksekgonul2023attention,yan2026abstention}. The question we ask is narrow on
purpose. We do not ask whether attention \emph{explains} a prediction, a question that has been
debated without a settled answer~\citep{jain-wallace-2019-attention,wiegreffe-pinter-2019-attention}.
We ask whether it \emph{decides}: whether ranking entities by attention lowers the error rate of what
is delivered beyond what confidence already achieves.

Our first measurement, on GENIA with \gliner{} base, was encouraging. Ranking predictions by the attention they receive
removed \rrGeniaMassBase\% of the risk of abstaining at random, that is, of choosing at random which predictions to send
for review; confidence removed \rrGeniaConfBase\%. Before combining the two, we had to know what the \rrGeniaMassBase\%
measured, and that meant taking seriously that attention is a fixed budget. Every token spends exactly one unit of
attention over the tokens it can see. A span of three tokens in a ten-token sentence receives more of that budget than a
span of one token in a forty-token sentence, for purely arithmetic reasons, whatever the model has learned. If span and
sentence length are related to correctness, attention will appear to predict errors even from a model that attends to
nothing in particular. To separate what the model contributes from what arithmetic contributes, we derived how much
attention a span would receive from a model indifferent to its tokens. The reference is exact and requires no fitting
(\cref{sec:null}): it is the fraction of the visible tokens the span occupies, which we call the \emph{geometric fraction}.

Measured against it, the evidence supports four findings, each reported in its own part of \cref{sec:results}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The attention an entity receives is mostly geometry} (\cref{sec:res-geometry}). The geometric fraction
  explains most of its variance in every family, and ranks errors as well as attention or better: within \encMassGeoMaxGap{} in
  area under the risk--coverage curve for \gliner{}, and better than attention for BERT and, with the direction set on
  calibration data, for the decoders. Compared with same-sized windows of the same sentence that are not entities, the
  \gliner{} extractors attend to entities slightly \emph{less}, the decoders slightly more (\cref{sec:res-windows}).
  \item \emph{The sign of attention's apparent value comes from the data} (\cref{sec:res-corpus}). Where long entities are
  more often correct, attention looks useful; where predictions in short sentences are more often wrong, it is worse than
  random. A prediction of the sign from the two lengths alone, declared before a third corpus was examined, held for
  \predPoneOk{} of \predN{} extractors; the full declared prediction held for \predConfirmed{} (\cref{sec:res-prediction}).
  \item \emph{Beyond geometry, attention adds almost nothing to confidence} (\cref{sec:res-confidence}). A small trace
  appeared within size classes in extractors not fine-tuned on the corpus; fine-tuning removed it, and no trace appeared in
  BERT, in the decoders or on the third corpus.
  \item \emph{What attention knows beyond geometry concerns the label, and confidence already knows it}
  (\cref{sec:res-errors}). Separated by type of error, attention ranks errors better than geometry only for errors of label,
  and in no case does it improve on confidence.
\end{enumerate}
We then asked whether these findings depended on how we read attention, on the particular extractor, on its training, or
on its architecture; they did not (\cref{sec:res-escapes}).

Our contributions are the following. (i) An exact, parameter-free reference for the attention a span receives, under
bidirectional and causal masks, that makes attention comparable across spans, sentences and models. (ii) Evidence, across
three families of extractors and three corpora, that the apparent value of attention for abstention in entity extraction
is mostly the value of that reference, and that its sign is inherited from how lengths relate to correctness, together with
the limit of that account on a corpus examined only after the prediction was declared. (iii) Evidence across readings,
architectures and types of error that attention adds no error information to the model's confidence. (iv) A protocol in
which every comparison, including the prediction for the unseen corpus, was declared before the measurement it concerns,
with the hashes of the declarations deposited publicly, and every outcome reported, the failed predictions included.
What remains open is stated in \cref{sec:open}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{What attention weights mean.} Whether attention tells us anything about a model is itself disputed. In text classifiers with a
recurrent encoder and a single attention layer, attention weights can be changed substantially without changing a
prediction~\citep{jain-wallace-2019-attention}, which weakens them as explanations without ruling them
out~\citep{wiegreffe-pinter-2019-attention}, and they predict the importance of inputs only noisily~\citep{serrano2019attention}; for token relevance, saliency methods are arguably the better
tool~\citep{bastings2020elephant}. Raw weights also leave out part of what a token contributes: the norm of the value vectors
they multiply~\citep{kobayashi2020attention} and the mixing of information across layers~\citep{abnar2020quantifying}; and,
within a head, once a sequence is longer than the head dimension, different weights can produce the same output, so the
weights are not identifiable~\citep{brunner2020identifiability}. Among the tests \citet{wiegreffe-pinter-2019-attention} propose is a comparison
with a model whose attention is frozen to uniform weights, applied to task performance, and \citet{clark2019bert} use a
uniform estimate informally to judge how much attention a token would receive by chance. Our reference applies the same idea
to the attention a span receives and gives it a closed form under both masks; we found no earlier closed form for it. This debate is about
explanation. We borrow its object and change the question to one with an operational answer: whether attention improves a
decision.

\paragraph{Where attention goes regardless of content.} Much attention goes to positions that carry little content. BERT has
heads that attend to delimiters, to fixed offsets or broadly over the sentence~\citep{clark2019bert}, and trained decoders
place a large share of attention on the first token~\citep{xiao2023sinks,gu2024sink}, a pattern argued to protect the model
from excessive mixing~\citep{barbero2025first}. How that first token is treated changes conclusions drawn from attention
rows: \citet{papamichalis2026attentionrows} treat a row as compositional data, separate a sink term from a content term,
and find that keeping or dropping the sink reverses 17--47\% of verdicts on which of two heads is closer to a third, across
ten pretrained models. The attention mass we read, with the first token removed, is their content term renormalised, and the
geometric fraction is that term's value when content attention is uniform. We fixed that choice before
measuring, and scanned three treatments of it among the readings of \cref{sec:guard}.

\paragraph{Attention as a signal of error.} Attention has also been read directly as an error signal, mostly in generation:
attention to the tokens that constrain an answer predicts factual errors~\citep{yuksekgonul2023attention}, the ratio of
attention to the context and to generated tokens detects contextual hallucinations~\citep{chuang2024lookback}, selected heads
combined with token confidence improve hallucination detection~\citep{vazhentsev2025rauq}, and attention-based grounding
scores have been used to decide when a summariser should abstain~\citep{yan2026abstention}. In some of these
settings attention adds to confidence: combining attention with the model's probability is often the best predictor of
factual errors~\citep{yuksekgonul2023attention}, and removing attention from the detector of \citet{vazhentsev2025rauq}
degrades it. In the summarisation study the grounding score was competitive in one of the two models tested and weaker than
an embedding baseline in the other~\citep{yan2026abstention}. None of these compares the signal with a reference for how
much attention a position would receive given only how many positions could be attended; the closest is the length
normalisation and the subtraction of a running mean of attention in \citet{vazhentsev2025rauq}. Closest to us,
\citet{saghir2026twopathway} shows that white-box scores for detecting out-of-distribution inputs, raw attention entropy among
them, are confounded by sequence length and fall to near chance when length is matched; for attention entropy the dependence on
length is shown to grow as $\Theta(\log T)$. Our results, on a different task and
for errors rather than shifted inputs, agree and go further in two directions. We give the confound an exact form for each
span, which shows that it acts through the fraction of the context the span occupies and not through sequence length alone.
And we show that it can reverse a signal, not only inflate it.

\paragraph{Confidence in entity extraction.} The literature on confidence for structured prediction supplies the bar.
The confidence a structured predictor assigns to its output entities can be calibrated, with lower calibration error for
entity extraction among other tasks; the predicted entity's length is among the features the calibrator
uses~\citep{jagannatha2020calibrating}. For generative labelling with an encoder--decoder, aggregating a span's probability over the beam
(AggSeq) gives lower calibration error than the span's own probability on most of six datasets~\citep{hashimoto2024beam}; we
use it as a second bar attention must clear in our decoders. For financial entity extraction with the same
families we use, a BERT tagger and Qwen2.5 at 0.5B and 1.5B parameters, \citet{zheng2026financial} compare confidence signals
under domain shift; attention is not among them. Analyses that break extraction performance down by entity length, sentence length and other
attributes, and find that performance varies with them~\citep{fu2020interpretable}, motivate our strata; we found no standard taxonomy that separates label from boundary
errors, and define our own (\cref{sec:further}). In 7B decoder-only models fine-tuned as token classifiers, relaxing the causal mask in all or some layers improves
sequence labelling on several tasks, with gains that depend on the task and that were not found at small
scale~\citep{dukic2024lookingright}; we keep the mask, because our reference accounts for it exactly.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

To answer the questions of the introduction we needed four instruments: a way to judge how well a score
separates correct predictions from errors (\cref{sec:risk}); a way to read, from the attention matrices, how
much attention an entity receives (\cref{sec:reading}); the reference against which that reading can be
interpreted (\cref{sec:null}); and the scores attention has to beat (\cref{sec:scores}). We then describe how we
protected the comparisons from our own choices (\cref{sec:guard}), the extractors and corpora (\cref{sec:setup}), and three
further tests that probe the findings from other sides: windows of the same sentence that are not entities, a taxonomy of
errors, and a prediction for a corpus not examined before (\cref{sec:further}).

\subsection{Judging a score: what it means to lower the risk}
\label{sec:risk}

We model the following situation: an extractor has produced $n$ predictions, each a span of text with a label. A reviewer will not inspect them all. Instead, the predictions are sorted by a score $s$, where higher
means ``more likely correct'', and the top of the list is delivered without review. A good score puts the
correct predictions at the top and the errors at the bottom. Everything that follows measures how well a
score does this. What matters is therefore the order a score induces, not its values: a score need not be a calibrated probability to
be useful for review, which is why attention, whose values say nothing directly about correctness, can be compared with
confidence on equal terms.

The unit of analysis is a \emph{predicted} entity. Its loss is $\ell=0$ when an annotated
entity has exactly the same boundaries and label, and $\ell=1$ otherwise. Entities the extractor missed are not
ranked, because in this setting a reviewer can remove a delivered prediction but cannot recover one that was never
made.

If we deliver the top fraction $c$ of the ranked list, $c$ is the
\emph{coverage} and the \emph{risk} $r_s(c)$ is the proportion of errors among what was delivered. Plotting risk
against coverage, from the single top prediction to the whole list, gives the risk--coverage
curve~\citep{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective}. A score that ranks well keeps the curve low at small coverages, where only its
most trusted predictions are delivered. When several predictions share the same score, the curve is evaluated only
where the score changes, because a reviewer cannot deliver part of a group the score does not distinguish.

Since no deployment is committed to a single coverage in advance, we summarise each score by one number, the area under the whole curve, the \aurc{}, computed by trapezoids, with the risk of the first point extended
down to coverage zero. Lower is better. Its limitation is that it weighs every coverage equally, including very small ones no deployment
would use; the review load, defined below, complements it by fixing an operating point.

A score is only useful if it beats ordering the predictions at random.
Under a random order, every prefix of the list contains errors in the same proportion as the whole set, so the
expected risk equals the overall error rate $\varepsilon$ at every coverage, and $\aurc_{\text{chance}}=\varepsilon$.
We compute this floor exactly rather than by simulation. Comparing a score with this floor answers the first question one would ask of it,
whether ordering by it helps at all, and computing the floor exactly keeps simulation noise out of every comparison that follows.

Comparing extractors requires a common scale, because they differ in how many errors they make: a raw \aurc{} of
0.1 is excellent for one and poor for another. To compare them we report
\begin{equation}
  \rho(s) \;=\; 1-\frac{\aurc(s)}{\aurc_{\text{chance}}},
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
the share of the risk of random abstention that ranking by $s$ removes. When $\rho=0.34$, the delivered predictions
contain, averaged over all coverages, 34\% fewer errors than a random selection of the same size. When $\rho=0$ the
score ranks no better than chance, and when $\rho<0$ it delivers errors \emph{earlier} than chance would. The maximum
is reached by the ideal ranking, which delivers every correct prediction before any error, and it is below 1 whenever
there are errors at all.

In deployment the same question takes another form, because a deployment usually fixes the precision it needs. For a target
precision $q\in\{\declQualityGrid\}$, we find the largest coverage whose risk is at most $1-q$ and call what remains the
\emph{review load}: the fraction of predictions a human must still inspect. A target no coverage reaches is reported as
unreachable.

The two measures answer different questions. The \aurc{} asks whether a score ranks better on average over all coverages;
the review load asks whether it saves a reviewer work at the precision a deployment requires. A score can improve the first
without changing the second, when its gains lie at coverages whose risk is above the target, and we therefore report both.

To decide whether one score is better than another, we always compare the two on the same predictions,
through the difference $\Delta\aurc=\aurc(s)-\aurc(s')$ or the difference in review load. Anything a comparison needs to
fit---a weight, a regression, a direction---is fitted on a calibration part holding \declCalFrac\% of the sentences and
then applied, unchanged, to the remaining sentences, where every reported value is computed. Fitting and evaluating on the same predictions would reward any score with something to fit, and would favour precisely the scores with a fitted part, the combinations and the geometric residual. Uncertainty comes from
\declResamples{} bootstrap resamples. We resample \emph{sentences}, not entities, because entities in one sentence share
the same attention matrix and are not independent; resampling entities would make the intervals too narrow. A score
\emph{improves} on another only when the whole 95\% interval of the difference lies below zero.

\subsection{Reading how much attention an entity receives}
\label{sec:reading}

The intuition behind attention as an error signal is that the model treats an
entity it gets wrong differently from one it gets right. The most direct measure of how the model treats an entity is
how much of the sentence's attention goes to it. We made that the primary reading. It is the quantity an explanation by attention most directly appeals to, and, as \cref{sec:null}
shows, it has an exact reference; a reading without a reference could not be interpreted.

In the bidirectional extractors, the encoder produces for each sentence an attention tensor
$A\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times H\times T\times T}$, indexed by layer, head, query and key. We first average over all layers and
heads, obtaining $\bar A$, and only then take ratios; averaging ratios instead would give a head that barely attends to
the sentence the same weight as one that carries most of the attention. We run the encoder on the sentence alone, without the label prompt that \gliner{} adds when it predicts, so that the
keys are the sentence's own tokens. In trained transformers a single position, usually the first, tends to absorb much of
each row's attention without carrying a proportional share of content~\citep{clark2019bert,xiao2023sinks,papamichalis2026attentionrows};
leaving it in the denominator would compress every other value towards zero, so, as declared, we remove the first token
(\texttt{[CLS]}) from the set of keys $K$; the closing \texttt{[SEP]} stays in $K$. The
span's tokens $S$ are found by mapping its characters to the tokenizer's tokens. The \emph{attention mass received} is
\begin{equation}
  m(S) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{q}\sum_{j\in S}\bar A_{qj}}{\sum_{q}\sum_{j\in K}\bar A_{qj}},
  \label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
the share of all the sentence's attention, among content tokens, that lands on the entity.

An entity could also be processed differently through the attention
its own tokens \emph{pay}. We therefore read three further summaries from the rows of the span's tokens: the \emph{row
entropy}, which is low when those tokens focus on a few positions and high when they spread their attention; the \emph{row
maximum}, the largest share any single position receives; and the \emph{mass emitted}, the share of their attention that
leaves the span. These summaries test a different hypothesis: that the model's uncertainty
about an entity shows in how the entity's own tokens gather context, rather than in how much attention the rest of the
sentence gives them.

In a causal decoder, the token at position $i$ can only attend to the $i+1$ positions up to itself.
The decoder reads the sentence in its prompt and then writes the mentions and labels it extracts, and we read the attention
over the prompt. Because each row sees a different number of positions, each row is normalised over the positions it can see
before the mass on $S$ is averaged over rows. Here the first token is kept. It is the first token of the prompt, which opens with the word
\emph{Text} (Qwen2.5 adds no beginning-of-sequence token), and under a causal mask it is the one position every
row can see, and the reference below counts it. A generated mention is located in the sentence by exact match. A mention that
does not occur in the sentence has no span and no attention; it is always an error, and it is removed for every score alike
(\decHallucMin{} to \decHallucMax{} per model and corpus).

\subsection{The reference: what an indifferent model would give an entity}
\label{sec:null}

A reference is needed because a value of $m(S)=0.2$ means nothing on its own. It is large for a one-token entity
in a long sentence and small for a long entity in a short one. To know whether a model gives an entity more or less attention
than expected, we need to know what to expect.

The reference comes from an indifferent model, one that does not distinguish among the tokens a row can see. Formally,
which $k$ of the $|K|$ positions happen to form the span makes no difference to how attention is distributed. Each row still
spends its whole budget, but no position is favoured. The share that lands on the span is then, in expectation, the share of
positions it occupies:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[m(S)] \;=\; \frac{k}{|K|}.
  \label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
The argument uses only the fact that each row sums to one and the linearity of expectation. It holds for any model, any
sentence and any matrix that is a distribution in each row. It is not an approximation, and nothing in it is fitted to data. We
call $k/|K|$ the \emph{geometric fraction}: it is determined by two lengths, that of the span and that of the sentence.

The causal case differs only in what each row can see: under a causal mask, row $i$ sees $K_i$ positions, $|K_i|=i+1$, and the same argument gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[m(S)] \;=\; \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i\in Q}\frac{|S\cap K_i|}{|K_i|}.
  \label{eq:nullcausal}
\end{equation}
For a contiguous span starting at position $a$ (counting from zero) in a sentence of $T$ tokens, with all $T$ rows
as queries, the sum has a closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[m(S)] \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\Bigl[\,\sum_{i=a}^{a+k-1}\frac{i-a+1}{i+1} \;+\; k\,\bigl(H_T-H_{a+k}\bigr)\Bigr],
  \label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
where $H_n$ is the $n$-th harmonic number: rows inside the span see part of it, and every later row sees all of it among
$i+1$ positions. Now position matters as well: a span early in the sentence is visible to more rows and, among fewer
positions, receives a larger share. The row summaries have analogous references, reached by a row that spreads its attention evenly: $\log|K_i|$ for
the entropy and $1/|K_i|$ for the maximum. None of these references depends on the model's width, depth or number of heads,
which is what makes them usable for comparing models of different sizes.

A worked example makes the difference concrete: \cref{fig:null} draws both references for a sentence of 60 tokens. Under a bidirectional
mask the reference depends only on size: a four-token entity expects \exBidFour{} of the attention, four times the
\exBidOne{} of a one-token entity, wherever either sits. Under a causal mask position dominates: a one-token entity at the
start of the sentence expects \exCausalStartOne{}, more than four times the \exCausalEndFour{} of a four-token entity
starting at position 45. A decoder's attention can therefore look selective simply because of where entities occur, and
the causal reference is what removes that effect.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{img/fig_null.pdf}
  \caption{The attention an indifferent model would give a span in a 60-token sentence, as a function of where the span
  starts. Dashed: bidirectional reference (\cref{eq:null}), which depends only on the span's size $k$. Solid: causal
  reference (\cref{eq:closed}), which also depends on position. Computed from the formulas; no data.}
  \label{fig:null}
\end{figure}

Because every later comparison rests on these references, we verified them
numerically with the same functions the analysis uses to measure attention. We drew attention tensors whose rows are far
from uniform (gamma-distributed weights), and then, \nullPerm{} times, permuted each row's weights among the positions that
row may see. A permutation is exactly the indifferent model: each row keeps its values, and only which positions hold them
changes. Across \nullConfigs{} combinations of sentence length, span size and position, the mean measured mass matched
the reference to within \nullBidMaxZ{} standard errors for the bidirectional case and \nullCauMaxZ{} for the causal one.

With the reference we can remove geometry from attention and see what is left.
We use two versions: the \emph{enrichment} $m(S)/\mathbb{E}[m(S)]$, equal to 1 when an entity receives exactly its share and
above 1 when the model singles it out; and the \emph{geometric residual}, what remains of $m(S)$ after a linear regression on
$\mathbb{E}[m(S)]$. If the model's attention to an entity carries information about whether the extraction is correct, it
should appear in these deconfounded scores. The two versions remove geometry in different ways. Enrichment is a ratio and places every
entity on the same relative scale; the residual is a difference and keeps the absolute amount of attention. Using both guards
against a conclusion that depends on how geometry was removed.

\subsection{What attention has to beat}
\label{sec:scores}

Attention is only worth reading if it adds to what the extractor already reports, its confidence. For
\gliner{}~\citep{Zaratiana2024gliner} the confidence is the probability the model assigns to the (span, label) pair, a sigmoid output for each pair. For the
Qwen2.5 decoders~\citep{qwen2025} it is the geometric mean of the probabilities of the tokens generated for the mention and its label.
For BERT~\citep{Devlin2019}, which labels each word, it is the geometric mean, over the words of the span, of the largest
softmax probability at the word's first sub-token, the same definition as for the decoders.

The decoders' own probability is known to be a weak estimate of span
correctness, so we also compare with AggSeq~\citep{hashimoto2024beam}: we decode five beams, weight each by its
length-normalised probability renormalised over the five, and give each predicted pair the total weight of the beams that
contain it. The original weights beams by their unnormalised sequence probability; the length normalisation is our declared
adaptation. Attention would have to beat both.

To see how much of attention's ranking can be obtained without looking at attention, we
also rank by the span length $k$, the sentence length $T$, and the geometric fraction itself. None of these needs anything from
inside the model. They bound what attention can claim: if a ranking that never looks inside the
model does as well as attention, whatever attention offers can be had without it.

The practically relevant question is whether attention improves confidence when the two are used together. We standardise both with the mean and standard deviation of the calibration part, combine them as
$w\,z_{\text{att}}+(1-w)\,z_{\text{conf}}$, choose $w$ among $\{0,0.01,\dots,1\}$ to minimise the \aurc{} on calibration, and
apply that $w$ to the evaluation part. Standardising puts the two scores on one scale, so that $w$ measures their relative contribution rather
than their units. The test is deliberately generous to attention: the procedure is free to give it any weight,
including all of it. If attention held information confidence lacks, the chosen weight would reflect it. A weight of exactly zero
means the procedure preferred confidence alone.

\subsection{Protecting the comparisons from our choices}
\label{sec:guard}

A negative result invites the objection that a different choice would have found the signal. We answered this in two ways.

Every comparison in this paper, with its estimator and its decision rule, was written down
before the measurement it concerns: which layers and heads to average, how to treat the first token, which scores to compare and
what counts as an improvement. This prevents the analysis from being adapted to the result (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

A declared choice can still be the wrong one. We therefore evaluated, for \gliner{}
base on the validation partition, \periReadings{} readings per corpus: four directions (attention received, attention the span pays
to itself, their mean, and attention received per token), three treatments of the first token, sixteen selections of layers (all
together, each alone, and three ranges) and thirteen selections of heads (all together and each alone). The point of this scan is
not to find the best reading, which would be selecting on the data, but to see whether any reasonable reading behaves differently.

\subsection{Extractors and data}
\label{sec:setup}

We use two corpora whose entities differ in kind: GENIA~\citep{Kim2003genia}, biomedical abstracts annotated with biomedical terms, and
CoNLL-2003~\citep{tjongkimsang2003conll}, newswire annotated with names of persons, locations, organisations and
miscellaneous entities. In our test partitions the GENIA mentions are longer (\corpGeniaMeanW{} words on average,
\corpGeniaMulti\% with more than one word, \corpGeniaNested\% nested in or containing another mention; \nSentGenia{}
sentences) than the CoNLL-2003 ones (\corpConllMeanW{} words, \corpConllMulti\%, none nested; \nSentConll{} sentences,
document separators excluded). A third corpus, BC5CDR~\citep{li2016bc5cdr}, chemical and disease mentions in biomedical
abstracts (\corpBcMeanW{} words on average, none nested; \bcNtest{} test sentences in the sentence-split release we
used), was examined only after the prediction of \cref{sec:further} had been declared.

The extractors come from three families, which differ in how an entity is produced. \emph{Span scorers}: \gliner{} base and
large~\citep{Zaratiana2024gliner} (checkpoints \texttt{urchade/gliner\_base} and \texttt{urchade/gliner\_large}), and \gliner{} base fine-tuned on each corpus, which raises validation F1 from
\ftFoneGeniaBefore{} to \ftFoneGeniaAfter{} on GENIA and from \ftFoneConllBefore{} to \ftFoneConllAfter{} on CoNLL-2003.
Fine-tuning asks whether a weak signal is merely a symptom of an extractor that fits the corpus poorly: an adapted extractor
makes fewer errors, and a signal about its errors should, if anything, become easier to see. \emph{Token classifiers}: BERT
base cased~\citep{Devlin2019}, fine-tuned on each corpus with a fixed recipe from the range its authors recommend (learning
rate $5\times10^{-5}$, batch 32, three epochs, last checkpoint, no search), labelling each word at its first sub-token, as in the original
model~\citep{Devlin2019}, with BIO tags derived from the annotation; test F1 is \bertGeniaFone{} on GENIA, \bertConllFone{} on CoNLL-2003 and \bertBcFone{} on BC5CDR. On GENIA, whose
entities nest, BERT is trained on the outermost flat labels and its flat predictions are matched against the nested
annotation by the same strict criterion. \emph{Generative decoders}: Qwen2.5 with 0.5B and 1.5B parameters~\citep{qwen2025}, starting from
the base (not instruction-tuned) checkpoints, fully fine-tuned on each corpus to write out mentions and labels and decoded greedily. The decoders address two objections a
single architecture leaves open: that the result belongs to bidirectional attention, and that it belongs to small models.
The causal mask changes the reference, and the two sizes differ by a factor of three in parameters. On BC5CDR the
fine-tuned extractors reach validation F1 of \bcTunedFone{} (\gliner{}), \bcSmallFone{} and \bcBigFone{} (Qwen2.5).

For BERT, attention is read exactly as for \gliner{}: all layers and heads are averaged, and the first token, [CLS], is
removed from the denominator. All values are on the test partition unless stated.

\subsection{Three further tests}
\label{sec:further}

\paragraph{Windows that are not entities.} The reference of \cref{sec:null} is a model that attends to nothing in
particular. A stricter comparison is the model itself on spans that are not entities. For each predicted entity of $k$ tokens
we take every contiguous window of $k$ tokens of the same sentence that overlaps no annotated and no predicted entity, with
no sampling and nothing to choose. We divide the entity's enrichment (attention received over the reference) by the mean
enrichment of its windows, $r(e)$. If the median of $\log r(e)$ lies above zero with 95\% confidence, the model singles
entities out; below zero, it attends to them less than to other spans of the same size. We also compare the median of
$\log r$ between errors and correct predictions. Entities with no possible window are left out and counted
(at most \winNoWindowMax{} per extractor and corpus).

\paragraph{Types of error.} A pooled ranking can hide a signal specific to one kind of error. Each error receives exactly one
type, by precedence: a \emph{label} error has an annotated entity with the same boundaries and a different label; a
\emph{boundary} error overlaps an annotated entity by at least one character; a \emph{spurious} prediction overlaps none. For
each type we analyse all correct predictions together with the errors of that type, and repeat on that set the two central
comparisons: attention against the geometric fraction, and confidence combined with deconfounded attention against confidence.
The declared criterion for a finding is that favourable intervals concentrate in one type, above the chance rate, in more than
one extractor.

\paragraph{A prediction for an unseen corpus.} If attention inherits its sign from geometry, the sign can be predicted on a new
corpus from the geometric fraction, which needs only the predictions and two lengths. Before any extractor was run on BC5CDR,
we declared, for each of six extractors (\gliner{} base, large and fine-tuned; Qwen2.5 0.5B and 1.5B; BERT), in the fixed
direction: \textbf{P1}, the risk removed by attention mass (\cref{eq:rho}) has the sign of the risk removed by the geometric
fraction (the causal one for the decoders); and, for the bidirectional extractors, \textbf{P2}, the two rankings differ by at
most \encMassGeoMaxGap{} in \aurc{}, the largest difference observed for \gliner{} on GENIA and CoNLL-2003. A prediction is
confirmed when P1 holds, and P2 where it applies. We also declared how the outcome would be read: all six confirmed would be a
replication, five a replication with a named exception, fewer a mechanism that, as stated, does not transfer to this corpus.

All three tests reuse the partitions, the bootstrap over sentences and the decision rules of the declarations they extend,
and remeasured predictions had to match the original measurement line by line before any analysis ran (\cref{app:protocol}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Each part of this section reports one finding. The first two concern what attention is; the next two, where its
apparent value comes from and whether that account carries to a new corpus; the last three, whether anything remains
once geometry is set aside, and whether the answer depends on our choices.

\subsection{The attention an entity receives is mostly geometry}
\label{sec:res-geometry}

The first measurement showed attention removing \rrGeniaMassBase\% of the risk on GENIA. Before interpreting that number
we needed to know how much of the attention an entity receives is simply its share of the budget.

Most of it is that share. For the three \gliner{} extractors, the geometric fraction of \cref{eq:null} explains the
attention mass with $R^2$ between \encRsqMin{} and \encRsqMax{} (\cref{tab:aurc}); for BERT, between \bertRsqMin{} and
\bertRsqMax{} on the three corpora; for the decoders, the causal reference of \cref{eq:nullcausal} explains
\decRsqMin--\decRsqMax{} of it. The deviations from the reference also have a consistent direction: in \gliner{} the median
entity receives only \encObsExpMin--\encObsExpMax{} of its geometric share.

Two quantities can be closely correlated and still rank errors differently, because ranking depends on the small
differences a correlation averages over; the residual variance could hold exactly the error signal we were looking for.
The decisive comparison is therefore in \cref{fig:mechanism}, which plots, for every extractor and corpus, how well the
geometric fraction ranks errors against how well attention does, both relative to chance. For \gliner{} the points fall on
the diagonal: the two rankings produce the same \aurc{} to within \encMassGeoMaxGap{} on GENIA and CoNLL-2003. They beat
chance together on GENIA and lose to it together on CoNLL-2003. For BERT, the third family, attention ranks errors
\emph{worse} than the fraction on all three corpora, by \bertCtwoMin{} to \bertCtwoMax{} in \aurc, with every interval above
zero. For the decoders the picture depends on the direction in which a score is read. Read in the fixed direction used for
the bidirectional extractors, higher values ranked as more likely correct, both scores are worse than chance and attention
is slightly less bad than the causal fraction (by \decRawGapMax{} to \decRawGapMin{} in \aurc). The decoder declarations
instead set each direction on the calibration part; it was reversed at all four points, both scores then beat chance, and
attention ranks errors \emph{worse} than the causal fraction, by \decCalGapMin{} to \decCalGapMax{} with all
\decCalGapAdv{} intervals above zero (\cref{tab:aurc}). In no family and in neither reading does attention carry an
advantage over geometry that would matter. Whatever attention contributed to the \rrGeniaMassBase\% we started from, the
lengths of the span and the sentence contributed as well, without the model.

\subsection{Against other spans of the same sentence, entities differ little}
\label{sec:res-windows}

That an entity receives less than its geometric share does not yet say whether the model treats entities differently from
other text: any span might receive less. The windows of \cref{sec:further} compare each entity with spans of the same size
in the same sentence that are not entities (\cref{tab:windows}). For the \gliner{} extractors the median $\log r$ lies
below zero in all \winEncN{} combinations: entities receive \winEncPctMin--\winEncPctMax\% \emph{less} enrichment than
same-sized windows of their sentence. Most of the shortfall of the previous section is therefore shared by any span; what
is specific to entities is small and negative. The decoders go the other way: in all \winDecN{} combinations entities
receive \winDecPctMin--\winDecPctMax\% more than their windows.

Singling entities out is not the same as knowing whether they are right, and the last column of \cref{tab:windows} compares
errors with correct predictions. For \gliner{} the difference is null on GENIA and slightly positive on CoNLL-2003 for base
and large, errors being singled out a little more. For both decoders on CoNLL-2003, errors are singled out markedly
\emph{less} than correct predictions ($\winDecConllJthreeMax$ and $\winDecConllJthreeMin$ in median $\log r$, intervals
excluding zero). This is the clearest difference between errors and correct predictions we found in attention. It was not
declared as a comparison with confidence, and we do not know whether confidence already contains it (\cref{sec:open}).

\subsection{The sign comes from how lengths relate to correctness}
\label{sec:res-corpus}

If attention is geometry, the sign of its usefulness should come from somewhere other than the model. On CoNLL-2003,
ranking by attention was worse than random, which is difficult to explain for a signal about the model's errors. The
explanation was testable: the sign should follow from how the two lengths that define the fraction, the span's and the
sentence's, relate to correctness in each corpus, without looking at attention at all. We ranked the predictions by span
length alone and by sentence length alone, and measured how much of the risk each removes (\cref{eq:rho}).

For the three \gliner{} extractors, the corpora differ in exactly the way that explains the reversal (\cref{fig:risk}). On
GENIA, longer entities are more often correct, and ranking by span length removes \rrSizeGeniaMin--\rrSizeGeniaMax\% of the
risk, while sentence length is nearly uninformative (\rrSentGeniaMin--\rrSentGeniaMax\%). The geometric fraction is then
dominated by span length and helps (\rrGeoGeniaMin--\rrGeoGeniaMax\%). On CoNLL-2003 the informative length is the
sentence's: predictions in longer sentences are more often correct, and sentence length alone removes
\rrSentConllMin--\rrSentConllMax\% of the risk, while span length is inconsistent across extractors ($\rrSizeConllMin$ to
$\rrSizeConllMax$\%). Because the geometric fraction divides by the sentence length, it places entities from \emph{short}
sentences at the top of the ranking, precisely the ones more often wrong, and it is worse than random for all three
extractors ($\rrGeoConllMin$ to $\rrGeoConllMax$\%). Attention follows it. BERT repeats the pattern: both the fraction and
attention beat chance on GENIA and are worse than chance on CoNLL-2003 (\cref{tab:aurc}).

The decoders make the same point from the other side. On GENIA their long predictions are \emph{not} more often correct:
ranking by span length is slightly worse than chance (\cref{tab:aurc}). Accordingly, read in the fixed direction, the causal
fraction does not help there either, and neither does attention; both are worse than chance on both corpora. The calibration
part of each decoder detects this and reverses both scores; once reversed, both beat chance, the causal fraction removing
\rrDecGeoCalMin--\rrDecGeoCalMax\% of the risk and attention \rrDecMassCalMin--\rrDecMassCalMax\%. The relation that sets the
sign belongs to the predictions being ranked, which depend on the corpus and on the extractor, and attention follows it in
every case.

The same model, reading the same kind of entities, can therefore appear to have informative or harmful attention depending
only on how lengths are distributed among its correct and incorrect predictions. This also reconciles our result with reports
that attention predicts errors: on a corpus where the correct entities are the long ones, attention will look useful.

\subsection{Beyond geometry, attention adds almost nothing to confidence}
\label{sec:res-confidence}

Confidence is the bar any score must clear, because it is free: the extractor already reports it. The question is whether
attention, and in particular attention with geometry removed, improves on it.

On its own, attention is far below confidence, as \cref{fig:risk} shows by placing every reading of attention next to
confidence and to the lengths. Confidence removes \rrEncConfMin--\rrEncConfMax\% of the risk for \gliner{} and
\rrDecConfMin--\rrDecConfMax\% for Qwen2.5. Attention mass removes between $\rrEncMassMin$ and \rrEncMassMax\% for \gliner{}
and between $\rrDecMassMin$ and $\rrDecMassMax$\% for Qwen2.5 in the fixed direction (\rrDecMassCalMin--\rrDecMassCalMax\% in
the calibrated one). In all \nAttCells{} combinations of extractor and corpus, every attention summary falls at least
\rrAttBelowConfMin{} percentage points short of confidence. The strongest of them, the causal row entropy on CoNLL-2003,
removes \rrDecEntConllMin--\rrDecEntConllMax\% against \rrDecConfConllMin--\rrDecConfConllMax\% for confidence.

A weak signal can still help a strong one if it carries different information. This is what the combination of
\cref{sec:scores} tests, with the procedure free to give attention any weight. For \gliner{}, the combination of confidence
with deconfounded attention changes the \aurc{} of confidence by at most \cthreeMaxAbs, about \cthreeMaxRel\% of its value,
and in \cthreeZeroPairs{} of the \cthreePairs{} combinations of extractor and corpus the chosen weight on attention is exactly
zero. For BERT the chosen weight is exactly zero on all three corpora, and on BC5CDR the combination improves on confidence
for none of the six extractors. In deployment terms, for the extractor fine-tuned on GENIA, reaching 90\% precision requires
reviewing \loadTunedGeniaConf\% of the predictions when they are ranked by confidence and \loadTunedGeniaMass\% when they are
ranked by attention: no prefix of the attention ranking reaches that precision.

Pooled results can hide a group of entities for which attention helps, and within entities of the same size a trace does
appear in the untuned extractors. \Cref{tab:strata}(b) repeats the combination within each stratum. Of the
\strataCthreeN{} stratum intervals, \strataCthreeFav{} improve on confidence, against about \strataCthreeChance{} expected by
chance among the \strataCthreeNonDeg{} that are not degenerate, and \strataCthreeAdv{} is worse. Both improvements concern
single-token entities on CoNLL-2003, for \gliner{} base ($\Delta\aurc=\cthreeKoneConllBaseD$) and large
($\cthreeKoneConllLargeD$). For the extractor fine-tuned on CoNLL-2003 the procedure puts no weight on attention in any
stratum. For the decoders (\cref{tab:strata-dec}(b)) attention receives no weight at all in \decStrataCthreeZeroW{} of the
\decStrataCthreeN{} stratum intervals, and improves on confidence in \decStrataCthreeFav{}, where chance would give about
\decStrataCthreeChance{} among the \decStrataCthreeNonDeg{} non-degenerate intervals: single-token mentions for the 1.5B
model on GENIA, a stratum of 170 entities whose interval is wide. The within-size information of \cref{sec:res-geometry} is,
for the most part, already in the confidence; where it is not, it belongs to extractors that have not been adapted to the
corpus.

The one place where raw attention did slightly better than the geometric fraction over all entities tells the same story:
GENIA, for \gliner{} base ($\Delta\aurc=\ctwoGeniaBaseD$, $[\ctwoGeniaBaseLo, \ctwoGeniaBaseHi]$) and large
($\ctwoGeniaLargeD$, $[\ctwoGeniaLargeLo, \ctwoGeniaLargeHi]$). For the extractor fine-tuned on GENIA the difference
disappears ($\ctwoGeniaTunedD$, $[\ctwoGeniaTunedLo, \ctwoGeniaTunedHi]$). Whatever attention carried beyond the entity's
size, a better-trained extractor no longer shows it.

\subsection{What attention knows beyond geometry concerns the label}
\label{sec:res-errors}

Errors are not all of one kind, and pooling them could hide a signal specific to one. We had expected, if anywhere, a
signal about boundaries: attention to an entity's tokens plausibly speaks to where it begins and ends. The taxonomy of
\cref{sec:further} divides the errors of the ten extractor--corpus combinations into \errNLabel{} label errors,
\errNBound{} boundary errors and \errNSpur{} spurious predictions (\cref{tab:errortypes}).

The expectation was wrong. Against the geometric fraction, attention ranks \emph{label} errors better in \errEoneLabelFav{}
of the ten combinations, all of them \gliner{} (by \errLabelMin{} to \errLabelMax{} in \aurc), and worse in none. For
boundary errors it is better in \errEoneBoundFav{} and worse in \errEoneBoundAdv{}; for spurious predictions, better in
\errEoneSpurFav{} and worse in \errEoneSpurAdv{}, in no consistent direction. Over all 30 intervals chance
would give about \errEoneChance{} favourable ones; the concentration in one type, in more than one extractor, meets the
criterion declared in advance. So there is one kind of error about which attention says something geometry does not: in
\gliner{}, whether a correctly bounded span was given the wrong label.

The confidence already says it. Combined with confidence on each type, deconfounded attention improves on confidence in
\errEtwoFav{} of the intervals and is worse in \errEtwoAdv; for label errors, the weight on attention is exactly zero in
\errEtwoLabelDeg{} of the ten. The information is real, and it is redundant.

\subsection{Could the conclusion be an artefact of our choices?}
\label{sec:res-escapes}

A negative result is only as strong as the objections it withstands. Four were natural, and we tested each.

One objection is that attention was read the wrong way. We averaged all layers and heads, and read the attention an entity
receives. Perhaps the signal lives in one layer, one head, or in the attention the entity pays. Across the \periReadings{}
readings of \cref{sec:guard}, each combined with confidence on validation data, the chosen weight on attention was exactly
zero in \periGeniaZeroWeight\% of readings on GENIA and \periConllZeroWeight\% on CoNLL-2003. Each weight is fitted on one part
of the validation data and evaluated on the other, so each reading is judged out of sample; only the choice of the best
reading among thousands is optimistic. \Cref{fig:readings} shows the whole distribution. On CoNLL-2003 almost every reading
changes nothing. On GENIA most readings improve confidence slightly: the median change is $\periGeniaMedDelta$ and a quarter
of the readings improve it by more than \periGeniaQuartDeltaAbs. The best reading changed the \aurc{} of confidence by
$\periGeniaBestDelta$ on GENIA and $\periConllBestDelta$ on CoNLL-2003. These are the numbers of \gliner{} base, the weakest
extractor, whose residual disappears after fine-tuning. The readings also share the confound: their median correlation with
span length is \periGeniaSizeCorr{} on GENIA and \periConllSizeCorr{} on CoNLL-2003.

A second objection is that the mass received is only one summary. The row entropy, row maximum and mass emitted, which
describe the attention the entity's own tokens pay, never improve on confidence in review load, for the large extractor
(\famLargeAttFav{} of \famLargeAttN{} intervals below zero) or for the fine-tuned ones (\famTunedAttFav{} of
\famTunedAttN).

A third objection is that the extractor was too weak. Fine-tuning raised F1 substantially and, on GENIA, where the residual
had appeared, removed it rather than revealing more (\cref{sec:res-confidence}).

A last objection is that one way of producing entities is a special case. We used three. The token classifier, BERT, repeats
the bidirectional result with attention slightly worse than geometry (\cref{sec:res-geometry}) and no weight on attention in
combination. Causal decoders process text differently, and their reference is different too (\cref{eq:nullcausal}). For
Qwen2.5 at both sizes, the three attention signals are worse than confidence and worse than AggSeq in all
\decAttPooledN{} comparisons. Tripling the size of the model, from 0.5B to 1.5B parameters, changes none of them.

\section{What the evidence establishes, and what remains open}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What is established}

The chain of results supports a specific conclusion. In entity extraction, the scalar summaries of attention one would
first reach for inform abstention largely through the geometric fraction, the share of the context an entity occupies.
Their apparent usefulness, and on the two corpora where it was found even its sign, is inherited from how lengths relate to
correctness among the predictions being ranked. What they carry beyond geometry concerns, in one family, the label of a
correctly bounded span, and it is already contained in the model's confidence. This holds for a span scorer, a token
classifier and a generative decoder, which produce entities in three different ways.

The conclusion is confined to this setting. In generation, where attention has been combined with confidence to
detect factual and contextual errors, it has been reported to help~\citep{yuksekgonul2023attention,vazhentsev2025rauq}, and our
results do not bear on those reports beyond suggesting the comparison with a geometric reference. These results run against an intuition that has guided much of the field for nearly a decade: that attention, the component
on which transformers are built, also carries usable information about what a model gets right. Our evidence is narrower
than that intuition. It concerns the scalar summaries of attention one would use to rank extracted entities, and within that
scope the information attention seemed to carry was in the lengths. Two lines of work reach compatible conclusions by other
routes. \citet{saghir2026twopathway} finds that white-box scores for detecting shifted inputs are confounded by sequence
length, and bounds the growth of attention entropy with length; we give the confound an exact form for each span, which shows that it acts through the fraction of the context the
span occupies, and that it can reverse a signal and not only inflate it. \citet{papamichalis2026attentionrows} show that
one-number summaries of an attention row mix how much attention the first token absorbs with how the rest is divided among
the content tokens; the attention a span receives likewise mixes how much of the context the span occupies with how the model
divides its attention beyond that, and the reference of \cref{sec:null} is what separates the two.

The account does not transfer unchanged. On a corpus examined only after the prediction was declared, the sign was predicted
for five of six extractors and the equivalence held for one of the three bidirectional extractors to which it applied. We read
this as a limit on the claim that attention \emph{equals} geometry, not on the claim that it adds nothing to it: every
departure beyond the bound went against attention.

\subsection{What remains open}
\label{sec:open}

The results do not show that the network holds no information about its errors, and several questions are left for the
next stages of this work.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Decoder errors receive less attention than correct predictions on newswire.} Relative to windows of their own
  sentence, both decoders single out errors on CoNLL-2003 markedly less than correct predictions (\cref{sec:res-windows}).
  Whether this difference is already in the decoders' confidence was not declared and is not known; it is the first
  comparison to run.
  \item \emph{Why the sign failed for one decoder on BC5CDR.} The causal fraction slightly helps and attention slightly hurts
  for Qwen2.5-1.5B. The two lengths do not explain this, and the risk removed by span and sentence length separately on that
  corpus would be the first step.
  \item \emph{Information that is not scalar.} Averaging over heads and layers, which our readings do, can destroy
  information spread across them. A reader trained on per-head attention, given the geometric fraction as an input, would
  test this. It would give up the exact reference, because its weights are fitted to the data. The same holds for hidden
  states and output scores, which have no exact reference at all; we study them separately.
  \item \emph{Errors made on purpose.} All errors we analysed are the model's own. Perturbing correct entities, by moving
  boundaries or changing labels, would ask whether attention responds to an error when the error is known, and needs a design
  with parameters of its own.
  \item \emph{Other tasks, domains and scales.} The evidence comes from entity extraction in two domains, biomedical and news,
  and from models of up to 1.5B parameters. The reference applies to any span, so the same comparison can be made for other
  span-level decisions and for larger models.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evidence comes from one task, three corpora and extractors of up to 1.5B parameters. Confidence is defined differently
for the three families, so each is compared with its own confidence and never across them. For \gliner{} the attention is read from a pass of the encoder over the sentence alone, not from the pass in which the extractor also encodes the label prompt and predicts; the two share weights but not inputs. In the decoders the first token, which is not a special token there, is kept in the denominator; the scan of first-token treatments was run for \gliner{} base only. The analysis of control windows
and of error types covers the ten extractor--corpus combinations of \gliner{} and Qwen2.5 on GENIA and CoNLL-2003, not BERT
nor BC5CDR. The comparisons were declared before measurement, but in a private repository; the released code verifies that
each result follows from its declaration, not when the declaration was written. Past dates cannot be established from a
private history, and we do not claim them. To make the declarations verifiable from here on, the SHA-256 of every
declaration, addendum and executable configuration was deposited on Zenodo (\preregDOI), and the registry was updated
before each new set of measurements; a single command checks the released files against the deposit. Several declarations
were made on the same two test sets, as each question followed from an earlier result; the third corpus is the one on which
the account was tested rather than found, and on it the account held only in part. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} lists every
departure from the declarations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We began by asking whether the attention an extracted entity receives could tell a reviewer which predictions to check. It
first appeared that it could. Asking what attention a model would pay to an entity if it paid no particular attention to it
led to an exact reference, and against that reference the apparent signal turned out to be mostly the share of the sentence
the entity occupies. That share predicts correctness on one corpus and anti-predicts it on another, and attention followed it
in both. With geometry set aside, attention added almost nothing to the extractor's own confidence, whichever way we read it,
whichever extractor we used, and whether entities were scored, labelled word by word or generated. On a corpus we had not
examined, the account predicted the sign for most extractors but not the full outcome; what held without exception was that
attention never ranked errors better than geometry and never improved confidence.

The practical lesson is simple, and the comparison that supports it costs nothing to run: before treating attention as
evidence about an error, compare it with the geometric fraction, which is computed from two lengths. Whether attention is
explanation has been argued both ways~\citep{jain-wallace-2019-attention,wiegreffe-pinter-2019-attention}. For deciding when
an extractor should abstain, what attention showed us was mostly a shadow on the cave wall: the outline of the share of the
sentence an entity occupies, which can be measured directly, from two lengths, without looking at attention at all.

Code, declarations and measured tables (without corpus text) are available at \repoURL{}, archived as \codeDOI. Every number,
table and figure in this paper is regenerated from them by a single command and compared byte for byte with the published
version. The twelve fine-tuned weights are archived under the licences they inherit, as \weightsDOIgliner{} (\gliner{},
CC-BY-NC-4.0), \weightsDOIqwen{} (Qwen2.5, Apache-2.0) and \weightsDOIbert{} (BERT, Apache-2.0), each with a content
fingerprint that the repository checks before any measurement.

\section{Pre-registration and analysis protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

The study was conducted under a series of declarations, each written as executable configuration before the measurement
it governs and hashed in two parts: what produces the measured table, and what analyses it. The analysis code refuses a declaration whose hash differs
from the signed document. Rules of the protocol are versioned, and each declaration is analysed under the rules of the version it was signed under.
This paper uses the declarations on attention; declarations on hidden states and output scores, measured in the same runs, are reported separately.

The declared comparisons used in this paper are the following. Descriptive: $R^2$ of attention mass against the reference, and the median of observed over expected.
Attention mass against the geometric fraction (\aurc). Confidence combined with enrichment against confidence (\aurc). Attention mass, enrichment, and
their combination with confidence against confidence (review load at each target precision). Row entropy, row maximum and mass emitted against
confidence (review load). For the decoders, the attention signals against confidence and against AggSeq. Each comparison is also reported by span
length ($k=1$, $2$, $3$--$4$, $\ge5$) and for nested versus flat entities.

When we count intervals, an interval is favourable when it lies below zero, adverse when above, and degenerate when both bounds are zero (the
combination chose weight zero, or neither score reaches the target). Under no difference, a non-degenerate interval is favourable with probability
0.025; intervals within a declaration share predictions, so this calibrates scale and is not a test. \Cref{fig:tallies} gives the count for every
declared comparison on attention.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/fig_tallies.pdf}
  \caption{Outcome of every declared interval on attention, by comparison. Counts to the right; dotted line: the 2.5\% chance rate of a favourable
  interval.}
  \label{fig:tallies}
\end{figure}

The later declarations extend this protocol. Two of them add new instruments on the original ten extractor--corpus
combinations: control windows and the taxonomy of errors (\cref{sec:further}). One declares the prediction for BC5CDR and its
reading, and one fixes the recipe for BERT; each new extractor--corpus combination then has a declaration of its own, a copy
of an earlier one with only the model or corpus changed. The control windows and the error types need data the original
tables did not keep (the window positions, and each predicted span with its label), so the predictions were remeasured; before
any analysis, the remeasured per-entity loss had to equal the original line by line, and it did for all ten combinations.

The departures from the declarations, in the order they occurred, were these:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The pilot (bootstrap over entities) preceded the adoption of the sentence-level bootstrap. Under the current analysis its estimates and
  verdicts are unchanged and its interval bounds move in the third decimal. It showed attention beating random abstention on GENIA
  ($\pilotFloorGeniaD$, $[\pilotFloorGeniaLo, \pilotFloorGeniaHi]$) and losing to it on CoNLL-2003 ($\pilotFloorConllD$,
  $[\pilotFloorConllLo, \pilotFloorConllHi]$).
  \item The row-summary declarations for the fine-tuned extractors were signed before, but measured after, the result of the same design on
  \gliner{} large was known.
  \item An addendum written after signature, and before any decoder result was computed, fixed which decoder quantity each name denotes, adopted
  the causal reference, and removed generated mentions absent from the input for every score alike.
  \item The exact-zero weights behind degenerate intervals depend on whether a score's direction is fixed on calibration data; the verdicts do not.
  \item In the remeasurement, the per-entity loss matched exactly; the confidence of the extractor fine-tuned on CoNLL-2003 differed in
  11 of 5{,}839 entities by at most $3\times10^{-5}$, from non-deterministic arithmetic on the accelerator. The guard was declared on the loss,
  and the confidence used in every comparison is the original one.
  \item The declaration for \gliner{} base on BC5CDR was copied from a template for a model with 24 layers, which \gliner{} base does not have,
  and could not be run. It was replaced, before any result on that corpus was seen, by a copy of the template for the 12-layer model with only
  the corpus changed.
  \item The prediction declaration lists BC5CDR partitions one sentence shorter than those loaded (5{,}228, 5{,}330 and \bcNtest{} sentences);
  the loaded files are the ones whose hashes the declaration fixes.
  \item The BERT recipe applied weight decay to every parameter, including biases and normalisation weights, which a common convention exempts;
  the declaration does not specify the exemption, and the model was not retrained.
\end{itemize}

Declared comparisons of the further tests are reported in \cref{tab:windows,tab:errortypes,tab:prediction}; \cref{fig:tallies}
counts those of the original declarations.
